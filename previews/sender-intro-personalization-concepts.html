The sender intro should say what label is being made — not just what we'll ask

Today /s/<code> opens on a headline, a city, and a five-item list of questions. Everything the creator already decided — the parcel, the ship-from address, the speed they're paying for — is invisible until the review screen, five taps later. Three options below, each shown at four levels of preconfiguration.

Four levels of preconfiguration

These come straight out of planSenderSteps() — a question is skipped when the link answers it. Every option below is drawn at all four, because an option that only looks good on a fully-specced link is not an option.

Option A — The shipment card, up front

reuses ShipmentDetailsCard; blanks are the questions

Built 2026-08-26. This is what src/components/sender/SenderStepIntro.tsx now renders. Two changes from the concept below: the headline is "Sarah Chen shared a prepaid shipping label with you", and the card carries a fifth full-width cell — PREPAID UP TO $25.00 — on any link with a cap. The cap is a deliberate exception to "the payer's money is not the sender's business": it is not what the recipient is spending, it is the budget they granted, and a sender who can see it can understand why an over-budget parcel gets turned away at the rates step. Exact rates stay hidden. The "three questions, then…" line is gone.

The idea. Show the sender the same 2×2 card the creator saw before they paid, and the same one the sender sees again on the review step — except here it's half-empty, and the empty cells are the questions. The creator's copy of this card says "Sender fills in." This is the mirror: "You'll add this." The numbered "How it works" list goes, because the card already names the open questions, in the same order, and stating them twice was the problem.

Cost to build: one afternoon. ShipmentDetailsCard already takes cells and already renders a deferred italic state. What it's worth. This is the only option that answers the literal question in the brief — what label will be generated — with the thing itself. It also carries through the flow: the same block can sit above every step, filling in as the sender goes, which replaces the progress bar that was removed on 2026-08-24 with something that shows decisions rather than position. That is the same argument the review-card change already won.

The risks, in order. 1. New construct — a LabelPreview component with no existing sibling, so it has to clear the Rule 6 bar on its own. 2. The barcode must read as unassigned, or someone screenshots this page and tries to ship it; keep it grey and keep the caption. 3. Parcel has no home on a real label, so it needs a bolted-on strip. 4. The masked street invites "why can't I see it?" — which is fine if answered, bad if left hanging.

Option C — One sentence, then what's left

no card, no list — a fact and a short checklist

The idea. Say what the shipment is in one line of plain English, then list only what the sender still owes. Detail hides behind a disclosure. This is the smallest possible change and the closest to the paradigm the flow already argues for elsewhere: state the decisions, don't narrate the position. When the link carries nothing it degrades to almost exactly today's screen — which is the right floor.

Where it breaks. Prose scales badly upward — the L3 sentence is already four clauses, and putting the parcel behind "See the details" means the sender may never look at the dimensions someone else guessed for the box in their hands. That's not just a nicety: a wrong parcel spec is a wrong rate, and the sender is the only person who can catch it. Options A and B put the box on the first screen; C hides it exactly when it's most worth showing.

Recommendation: build A, borrow C's headline, keep B on the shelf. A is free — the component, the cell shape, and the deferred-state styling all exist, and it makes the sender's first screen and last screen the same object, which is the single best argument for it. Take C's personalized headline ("Sarah Chen prepaid a label to Austin, TX") instead of A's generic one, and take C's footer line for the count of remaining questions, so the numbered list can go without losing the "you'll be asked things" signal. B is the better product and the more expensive one; it earns its build the day we want the sender intro to be a moment rather than a screen — and it's the only one of the three that answers the masked-street question out loud.

Part 2 — "No options for this one" should say why

the reason is already on the wire; the UI throws it away
Today, for every cause
No options for this one — the recipient's preferences are a little too picky for this package. Try adjusting the size or weight.
What it should say
Unfortunately this shipment doesn't work with Sarah's link, because … — and here is the one thing that would fix it.

Two things wrong with today's copy. It blames the recipient's taste ("picky") when the cause is usually a hard limit — a dollar cap, a carrier that won't take the box, a weight ceiling. And "try adjusting the size or weight" is an instruction to misdescribe the package. A sender who shaves a pound to get past the screen ships a mis-rated label, and the carrier's post-delivery adjustment lands on the payer's card — the exact surprise charge the adjustment emails exist to apologise for.

Contact support

No "ask Sarah" button — decided 2026-08-26. Three of these six causes are unfixable by the sender: a cap, a carrier filter, a speed filter. The tempting move is a nudge email to the creator, and the rails exist (_shared/resend.ts, _shared/email-templates.ts, dispatchNotifications) — but it buys a rate-limiting problem (anyone holding the URL could mail the creator repeatedly) and a privacy question (does the note leak the sender's parcel to the payer?) for something two people who already know each other will sort out by text. Every screen gets one action: Edit package details. The copy still names who set the limit, which is all the sender needs to know who to ask.

Order to build. 1. Declare messages on RatesResponse and render it — that alone fixes the worst case, where a 74 lb box gets told the recipient is picky. 2. Add blocked_by + quoted_count and split the copy. 3. Delete "try adjusting the size or weight" wherever it survives — it is the one line on this screen that costs the payer money. No step 4 — see above.
